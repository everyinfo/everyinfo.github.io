What is a Force Unit Converter?

The force unit converter switches between N (newton), kgf, lbf, and dyne.

How it's calculated

1 kgf = 9.80665 N, 1 lbf = 4.448 N, 1 N = 100,000 dyne

kg and kgf are not the same thing

Everyday speech treats 'weight' and 'mass' as the same, but strictly, kg is mass and kgf is force. Mass stays constant anywhere in the universe; weight (kgf or N) depends on local gravity. A person with 70kg of mass weighs about 1/6 as much on the Moon — but their mass is unchanged, even in zero gravity aboard a space station.

This distinction matters in engineering calculations: plugging kgf into F = ma introduces a 9.8x error if not converted properly.

Good to know

  • 1 newton is smaller than you might think — it's roughly the force of holding up an apple (about 100g), a handy mental anchor tied to Newton's famous story.
  • kgf is not an official SI unit, yet it still appears on older equipment nameplates and drawings, particularly in industries where legacy documentation persists.
  • The same confusion appears with torque — mixing up kgf·m and N·m introduces a 9.8x error, so always check bolt torque specifications carefully.
  • Pound also comes in two flavors: lb (mass) and lbf (force) — one of the most confusing aspects of US engineering units, which is also why the separate mass unit 'slug' exists.
  • Standard gravity, 9.80665 m/s², is a reference value — actual gravitational acceleration varies slightly by latitude and altitude, roughly 0.5% weaker at the equator than at the poles.

Frequently asked questions

Q. How many newtons is 1 kgf?

9.80665 N. In practice, 9.81 or 9.8 is commonly used.

Q. Is the kg on a bathroom scale mass or weight?

It's displayed as mass, but the scale actually measures force and divides by local gravity to calculate it.

Q. Should I use N or kgf?

The international standard is N. But kgf still appears on legacy industrial drawings, so conversion is sometimes necessary.
