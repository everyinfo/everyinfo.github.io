Board feet and lumber volume: nominal vs actual sizes

The board foot formula (T×W×L ÷ 12 or ÷ 144), why 2×4 lumber is not 2 inches by 4 inches, what that does to a quote, and how BF compares with cubic metres and the Korean jae.

Nov 26, 2026 · Design & Build · 8 min read

Two lumber yards can quote the same stack of 2×4s and differ by a third, and neither is cheating. One is pricing nominal board feet and the other actual. This guide covers the board foot formula, where nominal and actual diverge, and how BF lines up with cubic metres and the Korean jae.

One board foot is 144 cubic inches

BF = thickness(in) × width(in) × length(ft) ÷ 12  =  T × W × L(in) ÷ 144
  • 1 BF = 1" × 12" × 12" = 144 in³ = 0.0023597 m³
  • 1 m³ = 423.78 BF
  • 1 board foot = 1.4126 Korean jae (才)
  • 2 × 4 × 8 ft = 2 × 4 × 96 ÷ 144 = 5.333 BF (nominal)

The formula assumes inches for thickness and width and feet for length, which is why the divisor is 12 and not 144 in most US price sheets. The calculator accepts mm, cm, m or inch for each dimension separately, so you can mix a metric drawing with an imperial price list.

Nominal is not actual — and the gap is large

Softwood is sold by its nominal (rough green) size, but it is planed and dried before delivery. A 2×4 measures 1.5" × 3.5" in your hand. Board feet are still calculated from the nominal size in normal trade, so the price you see is for a volume that no longer exists in the board.

Actual BF = actual thickness × actual width × length ÷ 144. The 2×4 loses the most because both dimensions shrink by a quarter to a half inch.

This is why a quote can look 30% apart without anyone lying. Ask one question before comparing: is the board footage nominal or actual? Most published lumber prices are nominal. If a mill quotes actual volume, its unit price will look higher for the same pile of wood.

Hardwood uses quarters, softwood uses inches

Hardwood thickness is quoted in quarter inches: 4/4 = 1", 5/4 = 1.25", 6/4 = 1.5", 8/4 = 2". Hardwood is also sold rough and surfaced to order, so the thickness you pay for and the thickness you receive depend on how it is dressed. Widths are usually random, and the BF is measured on the board as it sits.

For softwood the naming is plain inches (2×4, 2×6, 2×8) and the actual size is fixed by the grading rules. Both trades use the same 144 cubic inch board foot.

What board footage costs in practice

2 × 4 × 8 ft, 100 pieces, $1,200 per MBF

  • 1 piece = 5.333 BF nominal
  • 100 pieces = 533.3 BF = 0.533 MBF
  • Cost = 0.533 × $1,200 = $640 (about $6.40 per piece)
  • If the yard quotes actual volume instead: 350.0 BF → $420 at the same unit rate

MBF means 1,000 board feet. Lumber is normally priced per MBF, so the unit price always looks 1,000 times bigger than the per-BF rate.

Waste belongs in the order, not the price. Allow 5–10% for cutting, defects and culling, and check the length you actually need — the calculator takes quantity, not an automatic waste factor.

Practical notes

  • Ask nominal or actual, every time. One question removes most of the gap between two quotes.
  • Round at the end. Convert the whole order to BF first, then apply the price. Rounding each piece to 0.5 BF adds up on large orders.
  • Watch moisture content. Green lumber shrinks as it dries. The BF does not change, but the finished dimension does, which matters for joinery.
  • Keep the dimensions with the price. "Price per BF" is meaningless without the section and length. The calculator's history keeps dimensions, quantity and total together.

Frequently asked

How many board feet in a 2×4×8?

5.333 BF on nominal dimensions (2 × 4 × 96 ÷ 144). Measured actual, the same board holds 3.5 BF.

Why is hardwood priced in quarters?

Rough hardwood is sold by thickness in quarter-inch steps: 4/4 is one inch, 8/4 is two inches. Surfacing removes material, but the price follows the nominal quarter.

Does the calculator convert to Korean jae?

Yes. Switch the volume unit on the result, or change the price unit, and the same dimensions are shown in jae as well as BF and m³.

How do I price a mixed order?

Convert each size to BF, add them, then multiply by the MBF price divided by 1,000. The calculator's history panel keeps one line per size so the mixed order can be totalled.
