T11 vs T12 vs US GMA pallet sizes, and how to load more per pallet

Compare T11, T12, EUR1 and GMA pallet dimensions, the boxes that fit on each, how many pallets a 20ft or 40ft container takes, and the stacking patterns that change the count.

Nov 25, 2026 · Logistics & Trade · 8 min read

Pallet standards differ by country, and the same carton can fit eight or nine to a layer depending on which pallet you put it on. This guide lists the six pallet sizes used by the CalcLex pallet calculator, the stacking patterns it compares, and how many pallets actually fit on a container floor.

T11 and T12 differ by 100mm — and that matters

T11 is a 1,100mm square; T12 is 1,200 × 1,000mm. The areas are almost identical (1.21 vs 1.20 ㎡). The difference is the shape.

  • A T11 square can be entered by a forklift from any side, and turning it 90° gives the same footprint. Two across a 2,352mm container uses 2,200mm and leaves 152mm.
  • T12 has a distinct length and width. Four across the 5,898mm length of a 20ft container uses 4,800mm and leaves 1,098mm wasted. Across the width it uses 2,000mm and leaves 352mm.

That is why a 20ft container floor takes 10 T11 pallets but only 8 T12 pallets. In a 40ft (12,032mm) both fit 20. The shorter the container, the more the square shape wins.

A 600×400 carton leaves 500mm on a T11 — not enough for a second one. Three per layer against five, over six layers, is 18 versus 30: a twelve-carton gap per pallet. If you export in the common 600×400×300 carton, check T12 or EUR1 before committing to T11.

Three stacking patterns and the "safe count"

  • Block stacking: every layer repeats the same orientation. Fast to build, but joints line up and the stack shears easily.
  • Interlock (alternate): odd and even layers alternate by 90° so layers lock into each other. Same count per layer, better stability.
  • Pinwheel: four cartons form a windmill around a centre. Depending on dimensions this can add boxes — or lose them.

The calculator adds one rule: any box that is supported by less than 50% of its base on the layer below is treated as unstable and removed, giving a "safe count". That is why interlocking can show the same theoretical maximum but a much lower safe number.

Six ways to load more per pallet

  1. Design the carton from the pallet. Standard pallets were not built to fit existing cartons; the KS series sizes (366, 275, 220mm) are 1,100 divided by 3, 4 and 5. Match them and the utilisation jumps.
  2. Add 3~5mm for stretch wrap. Wrapped cartons are wider than the measured box, and a perfect plan stops fitting on the floor.
  3. Keep overhang at zero. Anything outside the deck is unsupported and crushes. If it cannot be avoided, stay under 50mm and enter that value in the calculator's overhang field.
  4. Subtract the 150mm pallet height. From a 2,000mm limit, 1,850mm remains, which is 7 layers of 250mm boxes. Entering 0 for pallet height gives a wrong 8th layer.
  5. Check double stacking against container height. A 1,300mm loaded pallet doubles to 2,600mm — inside a 40HQ (2,698mm) but not a 40DV (2,393mm).
  6. Watch the payload. The 20ft preset allows 28,200kg against 26,460kg for a 40HQ. Half the volume but more weight: heavy cargo sometimes ships cheaper as two 20fts.

Frequently asked

Should I use T11 or T12?

T11 for domestic distribution in Korea, T12 for export and Asia in general. But the carton matters more than the standard: 400×300 fits 9 per layer on T12 and only 8 on T11; 600×400 fits 5 on T12 and 3 on T11. Put your carton in the calculator and switch the pallet type to compare directly.

Is pinwheel always better?

No. A pinwheel only forms when length + width fits inside the pallet side, and on T12 and GMA it often loses to block stacking. Compare all three patterns in the calculator and take the highest safe count.

Why do I fit fewer boxes than the calculator says?

The calculator uses outside carton dimensions. Wrap thickness, bulging cartons, deck gaps and door clearance each take a little back, so plan on one or two fewer boxes — or pallets — per load.
