Saltwater concentration and ratios, explained with pictures

Why the denominator is the whole saltwater, why adding water makes it weaker while the salt stays, and how the ratio rule a×d = b×c finds the missing number.

Nov 25, 2026 · Elementary Math · 6 min read

Saltwater problems are where children lose the most marks, almost always by dividing by the water instead of the whole solution. This guide explains what concentration measures, why adding water weakens it, and how a ratio writes the whole thing in one line.

Concentration = salt ÷ whole saltwater × 100

Concentration(%) = salt ÷ (salt + water) × 100
  • Salt 50g + water 450g → total 500g
  • 50 ÷ 500 = 0.1 → 10%
  • Dividing by the water (450) gives 11.1% — the classic mistake

Why the whole? Concentration measures how much salt sits in one cup of the mixture. Adding salt adds weight to the mixture too, so the base is always salt + water = the whole solution.

Working backwards to the salt

Salt = total × concentration ÷ 100
  • 10% solution of 500g → 500 × 0.1 = 50g of salt
  • Water = 500 − 50 = 450g
  • 20% solution of 300g → 60g salt, 240g water

Adding water: the salt stays, the total grows

This is the heart of the topic. Pouring water in does not make salt disappear. The amount of salt is unchanged and only the total grows, so the concentration falls.

10% solution of 500g plus 500g of water

  • Salt stays at 50g
  • Total becomes 500 + 500 = 1,000g
  • New concentration = 50 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 5% — exactly half

Doubling the water halves the concentration because the salt is constant and the whole doubled. Boil the water off instead and the total shrinks, so the concentration rises.

Careful: only about 26% dissolves

At room temperature (20℃) water dissolves about 36g of salt per 100g. 36 ÷ 136 = 26.5%, so about 26% is the saturation point. Enter 30% into the calculator and it notes that the extra salt will sit undissolved on the bottom. Maths problems can go to 100%, but a real experiment cannot pass 26%.

A ratio says two comparisons are equal

a : b = c : d means the comparison of a to b is the same as c to d. One rule always holds:

a × d = b × c
  • 2 : 3 = 8 : 12 → 2 × 12 = 24 and 3 × 8 = 24
  • 3 : 5 = 12 : ? → 3 × ? = 5 × 12 = 60 → ? = 20
  • Multiply the outer pair and the inner pair; the products are equal

It works whichever slot is empty: multiply the diagonal pair and divide by the remaining number. The kids' ratio calculator shows those steps one line at a time.

Using ratios on saltwater

10% concentration means salt : solution = 10 : 100 = 1 : 10. Written as a ratio, any size of batch can be built directly.

1 : 10 = ? : 200 (salt in 200g of 10% solution)

  • 1 × 200 = 10 × ? → ? = 20g
  • Water = 200 − 20 = 180g
  • Check: 20 ÷ 200 × 100 = 10% ✓

20g of salt in 180g of water makes 200g of 10% solution. The salt : water ratio 20 : 180 = 1 : 9 is worth remembering too.

Three practice problems

1. 30g of salt dissolved in 270g of water — concentration?

  • Total 300g → 30 ÷ 300 × 100 = 10%

2. How much salt and water in 400g of 15% solution?

  • Salt 400 × 0.15 = 60g, water 400 − 60 = 340g

3. 20% solution of 300g with 200g of water added?

  • Salt stays 60g, total 500g → 60 ÷ 500 × 100 = 12%

4. 4 : 7 = 12 : ? — find ?

  • 4 × ? = 7 × 12 = 84 → ? = 21

Frequently asked

Why is dividing by the water wrong?

Because concentration is the share of salt in the whole solution. Dividing by the water uses a smaller number, so the answer comes out too high: 11.1% instead of 10% for 50g salt in 450g water.

Why does the salt not change when water is added?

Water does not carry salt away. The dissolved salt stays in the beaker; only the total weight grows, which is exactly why "the salt amount does not change" is the key to every dilution problem.

What if the empty slot is in the middle?

It still works. For 2 : ? = 8 : 12, 2 × 12 = ? × 8 → ? = 3. The calculator solves any of the four slots.
