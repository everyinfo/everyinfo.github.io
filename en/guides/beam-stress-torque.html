Beam bending stress, second moment of area and torque

Separating force (N), stress (MPa) and moment (N·m), why I = bh³/12 grows eightfold when depth doubles, allowable stress and safety factor, and the torque / speed / power relationship.

Nov 26, 2026 · Design & Build · 10 min read

Most design-check errors are not arithmetic. They are unit mix-ups. Force (N), stress (MPa) and moment (N·m) look alike and behave completely differently, and the second moment of area reacts to depth cubed. This guide reproduces the calculator's own formulas by hand and shows where a factor of 1,000 appears.

Force, stress and moment are three different quantities

QuantitySymbolUnitMeaningExample
ForceFNPush or pull5,000 N
Stressσ, τMPa = N/mm²Force carried by each mm² of section10 MPa
MomentMN·mm or N·mRotational effect of a force500,000 N·mm
TorqueTN·mMoment turning a shaft30.01 N·m

1 MPa is exactly 1 N/mm². 1 kgf/cm² = 98,066.5 Pa = 0.098 MPa and 1 psi = 6,894.76 Pa.

Axial load (tension or compression)

σ = F ÷ A
  • F = 5,000 N, A = 500 mm² → 10 MPa
  • to kgf/cm²: 10 × 10⁶ ÷ 98,066.5 = 101.97 kgf/cm²
  • to psi: 10 × 10⁶ ÷ 6,894.76 = 1,450 psi

Bending

σb = M ÷ Z
  • M = 500,000 N·mm, Z = 5,000 mm³ → 100 MPa
  • Entering M = 500 N·m instead gives 0.1 MPa — 1,000 times smaller.

The second case is the dangerous one. Z is in mm³, so M must be in N·mm. A moment typed in N·m produces a stress 1,000 times too small and a design that looks comfortably safe.

The second moment of area follows depth cubed

For a rectangle, I = bh³/12 and Z = bh²/6. Doubling the width doubles I, but doubling the depth multiplies I by 8 and Z by 4. That is why the same material performs so differently upright and flat.

Rectangular sectionArea AI = bh³/12Z = bh²/6Radius k
50 × 100 upright5,000 mm²4,166,667 mm⁴83,333 mm³28.87 mm
100 × 50 flat5,000 mm²1,041,667 mm⁴41,667 mm³14.43 mm
50 × 200 depth doubled10,000 mm²33,333,333 mm⁴333,333 mm³57.74 mm

Laying the same 50×100 on its side cuts I to a quarter and multiplies deflection by 4 (δ ∝ 1/I). Doubling the depth multiplies I by 8 and Z by 4.

The practical reading is simple: equal area does not mean equal strength. I measures how far the material sits from the neutral axis, which is exactly why an I-beam concentrates steel in the top and bottom flanges and keeps the web thin.

Allowable stress and safety factor set the usable limit

A calculated stress needs a limit to compare against. That limit is the yield (or tensile) strength divided by a safety factor.

σallow = σy ÷ SF
  • Structural steel SS400: yield strength 245 MPa (KS D 3503)
  • SF 1.5 → 163 MPa · SF 2.0 → 122.5 MPa · SF 3.0 → 81.7 MPa
  • A working stress of 100 MPa means SF = 245 ÷ 100 = 2.45
  • If the specification demands SF 3, a 100 MPa stress needs a material above 300 MPa.

Static structures commonly use 1.5–2; cyclic, shock or vibrating loads commonly use 3 or more.

The safety factor is not padding. It bundles load-estimate error, material scatter, fatigue, corrosion and fabrication tolerance into one number, which is why repeated loading calls for a larger value than a static case.

Torque, speed and power: know one, get the other two

For a rotating shaft, power P (kW), speed N (rpm) and torque T (N·m) are locked together. The constant 9550 comes from 60,000 ÷ 2π (60 seconds per minute, 2π radians per revolution).

T[N·m] = 9550 × P[kW] ÷ N[rpm]  |  P = T × N ÷ 9550  |  ω = 2πN ÷ 60
  • P 5.5 kW at 1,750 rpm → 9550 × 5.5 ÷ 1750 = 30.01 N·m = 3.06 kgf·m
  • Angular velocity ω = 2π × 1750 ÷ 60 = 183.26 rad/s
  • Gear Z₁ 20 → Z₂ 60: ratio 3 → output 583.3 rpm, torque 90.04 N·m at 100% efficiency, 85.54 N·m at 95%
  • Power labels: 5.5 kW = 7.38 HP = 7.48 PS

A 3:1 reduction triples the torque but leaves power unchanged apart from losses. Mixing HP (0.7457 kW) with PS (0.7355 kW) introduces a 1–2% error, so read the motor nameplate unit before converting.

Top 6 things people get wrong

#MisconceptionCorrect basis
1Treating force (N) and stress (MPa) as the sameStress = force ÷ area. 5,000 N is a force; 10 MPa is what it produces over 500 mm².
2Dividing a moment in N·m by Z in mm³The stress comes out 1,000 times low. Convert M to N·mm.
3"Same area, same strength"I follows depth cubed. Flat orientation cuts I to a quarter and quadruples deflection.
4Using I and Z interchangeablyI governs deflection (stiffness), Z governs stress (strength). For one section, Z = I ÷ (h/2).
5"High torque means high power"Power = torque × speed. A low-speed, high-torque drive can draw less power than a fast one.
6Comparing stress with yield strength directly240 MPa on a 245 MPa steel is SF 1.02 — effectively a fail, not a pass.

Practical notes

  • Work in mm, N and MPa. Starting in that unit set gives stress in MPa with no conversion. The calculator has unit selectors — check them before typing.
  • Check deflection separately. A member can pass on stress and still vibrate. Raising I is the most direct way to cut deflection.
  • Check buckling for compression members. Long struts fail by buckling before stress. Use the radius of gyration k to get the slenderness ratio λ = L/k.
  • Use section tables for real profiles. The I-beam expression here is an approximation; rolled H-sections have tabulated I and Z.

Frequently asked

Is MPa different from N/mm²?

They are the same unit. 1 Pa = 1 N/m², so 1 MPa = 10⁶ Pa = 1 N/mm². That is why F (N) ÷ A (mm²) already reads in MPa.

Where is the section modulus used?

In bending stress, σ = M/Z. A larger Z lowers stress for the same moment. Use I for deflection and Z for stress.

How big is 30 N·m?

It is the torque of a 5.5 kW motor at 1,750 rpm — roughly 300 N (30 kgf) applied at a 0.1 m radius.

Why does a gearbox increase torque?

Power is conserved while speed drops. A 3:1 reduction triples the torque, minus gearbox efficiency, which the calculator assumes at 95% for 85.54 N·m.
