Pipe size, velocity and pump power selection

Why nominal bore is not the inside diameter, how doubling the diameter cuts velocity to a quarter and friction loss to about a thirty-first, Reynolds number, and sizing a motor from hydraulic power.

Nov 26, 2026 · Design & Build · 9 min read

"It is a 50A pipe, so the bore is 50 mm" is where losses start drifting by 20% or more. Nominal bore is a name; the inside diameter depends on the wall thickness. This guide follows the order in which velocity, loss and pump power are actually computed, and shows what doubling the diameter really buys.

Once flow is fixed, velocity is decided by area

Q = A × V  |  A = πD² ÷ 4
  • D = 100 mm (0.1 m) → A = π × 0.01 ÷ 4 = 0.007854 m²
  • Q = 50 m³/h = 0.013889 m³/s → V = 1.768 m/s
  • Same flow at D = 200 mm → V = 0.442 m/s (exactly a quarter)
  • Same flow at D = 50 mm → V = 7.074 m/s (four times)

Double diameter, quadruple area, quarter velocity. Friction loss then collapses: for the same 50 m³/h, the head loss over 100 m falls from 2.997 m to 0.097 m, roughly one thirty-first. Loss scales with velocity squared, which is why pipe sizing is the cheapest pump upgrade there is.

Pushing 50 m³/h through 50 mm costs 102 m of head per 100 m. Nobody designs that. Real projects set a target velocity and size the pipe backwards from it.

Recommended velocity is set by noise and erosion

The calculator notes supply 1–2 m/s, drainage 0.6–1.5 m/s and steam 20–40 m/s. Above 2.5 m/s, noise and pipe erosion become the design constraint rather than pressure drop.

Reynolds number tells you which friction law applies

Reynolds number is the ratio of inertial to viscous force. The calculator labels Re < 2,300 laminar, 2,300–4,000 transitional and above that turbulent, taking f = 64/Re for laminar flow and iterating Colebrook for turbulent flow.

Re = ρ × V × D ÷ μ
  • Water 20°C, D 0.1 m, V 1.768 m/s → 176,168 (turbulent)
  • Friction factor f = 0.0188 (Colebrook iteration)
  • hf = f × (L/D) × V² ÷ (2g) = 0.0188 × 1,000 × 3.126 ÷ 19.613 = 2.997 m
  • ΔP = ρ × g × hf = 998.2 × 9.80665 × 2.997 = 29.34 kPa (≈ 0.299 kgf/cm²)

Drop the same 100 mm pipe to 10 m³/h and velocity falls to 0.354 m/s with Re 35,234 — still turbulent, but the loss drops to 0.152 m per 100 m. One fifth of the flow costs about one twentieth of the loss.

Pump power steps up four times

Lw = γ × Q × H ÷ 6120  [kW]  |  Ls = Lw ÷ η  |  motor = Ls × K
  • Q 1 m³/min, H 30 m, γ 1,000 kgf/m³ → Lw = 4.902 kW (6.665 PS)
  • Efficiency 75% → Ls = 6.536 kW (8.886 PS)
  • Margin K 1.15 → 7.516 kW → select the standard 7.5 kW motor
  • Specific speed Ns = 1750 × √1 ÷ 300.75 = 136.5 → centrifugal (volute)

4.9 kW of useful work needs a 7.5 kW motor because efficiency and margin sit in between.

The constant 6120 comes from the units: 1 kW = 102 kgf·m/s, and 60 seconds make 102 × 60 = 6,120. That is also why flow goes in m³/min. If you know m³/h, divide by 60 first.

Specific speed identifies the impeller family: the calculator labels below 250 as centrifugal volute, below 450 as centrifugal turbine, below 1,000 as mixed flow, and above that as axial. Selecting double suction halves the flow entering each eye and lowers Ns.

Speed changes follow the affinity laws: 10% more rpm gives 10% more flow, 21% more head and 33.1% more power. That cubic response is why VFD control saves so much energy.

Top 6 things people get wrong

#MisconceptionCorrect basis
1"50A means a 50 mm bore"Nominal bore is a label. Outside diameter is fixed at about 60.5 mm and the bore varies with schedule. Calculate with the actual inside diameter.
2"Double diameter, half the velocity"Area goes up fourfold, so velocity is a quarter.
3Confusing flow rate with velocityFlow is m³/h (how much), velocity is m/s (how fast). The same flow runs faster in a smaller pipe.
4Leaving friction out of total headTotal head = static lift + friction loss + pressure head (+ velocity head). Omitting loss undersizes the motor.
5Treating hydraulic power as the motor ratingEfficiency (÷η) and margin (×K) multiply it by roughly 1.5.
6"10% more rpm, 10% more power"Power follows the cube: 33.1% more.

Practical notes

  • Start from 1.5 m/s. A sensible target for supply lines. Enter flow and diameter and check the velocity stays under 2 m/s.
  • Set the roughness. The default is steel at 0.045 mm. Stainless 0.015, PVC 0.0015 and cast iron 0.26 mm all shift the answer.
  • Add fittings separately. The calculator covers straight pipe only. Elbows and valves often add 20–40% to real system loss.
  • Check NPSH separately. Suction lift, water temperature and vapour pressure decide whether the pump cavitates — outside this calculator's scope.

Frequently asked

If I double the pipe, does the electricity bill fall to one eighth?

Loss falls to about one thirty-first, but the bill does not follow that ratio directly. Pump efficiency, the operating point and the head actually required all change. Recalculate shaft power for the new pipe and compare.

Why is 2.5 m/s the practical limit?

Noise rises, wall erosion accelerates and elbows and valves take concentrated impact. Water hammer at that velocity can crack pipework.

Why does double suction lower specific speed?

The impeller draws from both sides, so each eye handles half the flow. The calculator divides Q by two for the double-suction option.

Should velocity head be in the total head?

For accuracy, yes — add V²/2g at the discharge along with the losses. At low velocity it is small, at high velocity it is not.
